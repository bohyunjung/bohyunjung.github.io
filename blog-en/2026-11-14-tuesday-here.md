November 11th marked two years since I joined MongoDB. It's my fifth company, and only the second where I've stayed longer than two years.

There are many ways to mark a second anniversary. I decided to take stock of what I've been noticing in my work lately and write down what each of those things means to me.

### Sense #1: Localness

Something happened recently at an all-hands for the Builder Relations organization. Our new director opened the meeting with a greeting for a team spread across time zones. Good morning, good afternoon, good evening. Happy Monday! What an inclusive way to start. I appreciated it. Still, I typed "Tuesday here" in the chat, a small reminder that one of us was already a day ahead, past the date line.

In true Land of the Morning Calm fashion.

### Sense #2: Influence

The workshop content I built is being reused by local colleagues and community leaders in Sydney, Melbourne, Jakarta, Kuala Lumpur, and Taipei. It's hugely rewarding to know my work keeps working in rooms I'm not in.

As our APAC team has grown, I've welcomed three new colleagues this year alone and helped onboard them. I've also been working with the leader of the Seoul MUG (MongoDB User Group) for a year now. Giving people resources and support so their efforts can count for more has been more fun than I expected.

Two years is a long time in an industry that moves this fast. It's time to stop being the follower who learns quickly and says "yes" on reflex. I should have opinions of my own, set direction, and make an impact.

### Sense #3: Language

I joined a brainstorming session on the team's plans for next year. The topics deserved a multi-day offsite, but we had one hour. I sat in meetings like this for ten years in Korea, and sometimes I ran them. Even so, listening closely in a short window and then adding my own thinking in a way that lands is never easy. Doing it in English adds a whole other layer.

So what do I find where these senses overlap?

### Language and Localness

I speak Korean pretty fluently. Nobody checked during my hiring process, but the company got lucky. I assume the headcount for my role opened because the company takes the Korean market seriously and needed someone who could work in Korean. Global competitors also tend to struggle in areas where a Korean-only service already holds a big share. Under the right conditions, being a native speaker of a less widely spoken language works as both a barrier to entry and an advantage.

But a smaller language has its downsides too.

The obvious one first. At a global company headquartered in the US, major decisions and product development happen in English. Anyone who's browsed these companies' job postings knows this. Most roles in non-English-speaking regions exist for local sales and execution. Teams are called APAC, EMEA, and LATAM because they exist for their region. You rarely hear of "the North America team." The US and the UK are where company-wide organizations like product and design sit. And I hardly need to explain why global companies put their APAC headquarters in Australia, Singapore, and India.

Korea is a non-English-speaking region, which makes it a "market" from both a global and an APAC point of view. The roles global companies hire for here are mostly customer-facing work in Korean, for local execution.

Yet the people I measure myself against are leaders at the APAC and global level, more than execution specialists in the Korean market. That's why I, someone who still eats rice every day, stay hungry for English. (Ah, how I sometimes envy my Singaporean colleagues, who eat rice and grow up with both English and Chinese.)

Next year's plans will include investing in Japanese and Chinese to grow my leadership across APAC. I'll also keep looking for chances to throw myself deeper into English-speaking environments.

### Localness and Influence

A global company's gravity pulls toward the Atlantic, where headquarters sit, and that brings a certain frustration. Joining the meetings where big decisions get made means staying up late. Every document and recording is shared, but if you're not there live, it's hard to hear others or be heard. Unless I keep working at it, information reaches me at a lower resolution after passing from headquarters through the APAC regional office. The reverse is true too. Making my own work visible to everyone takes constant effort.

I see more and more Koreans who move straight to the US and make their mark there. Next to them, talking about limits from a fairly safe and familiar place may sound like a privileged complaint. It is. But I'm the kind of person who finds a way by adapting to the environment I'm in and stacking small optimizations, rather than taking one giant leap. Having my feet in a local market while staying connected to a global organization also shows me opportunities I wouldn't see otherwise.

What experience do I need now to step up into a larger role, say a Staff-level Developer Advocate leading APAC?

Once I accept the geographic and structural reality, the work gets clearer. I want to run as many experiments in Korea as I can across developer experience, education, and community. The best of them can become APAC standards and references, and then find their way into global programs. That track record is how I earn a voice in higher-level decisions.

This isn't about neglecting Korea. I think it's the only way Korea gets better represented in the global market. I have another vision too. For now I'll focus on closing this gap from inside a global company. But one day, with enough experience behind me, a Korean company may need me. When that happens, I hope to help Korean companies reach developers abroad, bringing experience that goes beyond local execution to designing programs at a global scale. I love the culture that raised me, and it's still home to my family.

### Influence and Language

When a survey asks which AI tools I use every day, I click "Others" and type "Grammarly" into the box that appears. My annual $144 renewal went through a few days ago.

I care about communication that's kind, clear, and well-ordered, so I put real effort into choosing words and structuring sentences. Working in English multiplies that effort, and Grammarly helps a lot. My English isn't yet where my Korean is.

Speaking English as well as I speak Korean only became a goal very recently. At one point in my life, my English goal was probably to call the front desk, order room service, and mention without fuss that my room had no hair dryer. Now I want to speak the language of technical and strategic leaders, fluently, in English. To keep learning is to keep raising the bar you hold yourself to, which means always feeling like you're falling short. That's why I feel the gaps in my English more than ever.

Maybe it's less about English now, and more about language itself.

Not long ago, ahead of a major company announcement, we had a change at the top. It could have been an unsettling time. Instead, I watched the leader who had guided the company for years and built it into what it is step back in and pull the organization together. Much of that work had to be done with words, reassuring people and calming nerves, and he's been doing it remarkably well. Over the past two years, there have been a few people whose language I wanted so badly I'd have swallowed them whole to get it.

I have a pet theory. Language amplifies the knowledge and experience a person has, and it sets the size of their influence and leadership. If you drew someone's abilities as an N-sided polygon, language would scale the whole shape rather than sit at a single vertex.

![On the left, a hexagon where language is one of six vertices, dented because only the language score is low. On the right, a full pentagon of five abilities, scaled outward by language as a multiplier.](/img/blog/2026-11-14-tuesday-here/language-amplifier-en.svg)

However good the shape, multiply every ability by zero and nobody cares about the shape. I doubt someone like that could sit across from customers and C-level executives on a global stage, or grow a business.

---

It's been a while since I wrote anything here.

Looking back on two years at MongoDB, I'm grateful for a time that let me dream like this.

So I'll keep pushing, keep listening, and keep trying to contribute. Oddly enough, feeling that something's missing may be a sign that one plateau in a staircase of growth is coming to an end. I'm curious what I'll be noticing in year three.

Tuesday here.
